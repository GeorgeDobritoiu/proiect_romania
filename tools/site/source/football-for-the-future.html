01 / THE PRINCIPLE

A system that develops players.
More than a team that wins.

Invest widely at the base of the pyramid, develop talent professionally, build a pathway to senior football and reinvest the value created.

A contribution to dialogue, open to collaboration with the Romanian Football Federation (FRF), county football associations, clubs, schools and local authorities. The proposal aims to strengthen existing efforts and test complementary solutions.

The stateWe propose that it fund public benefit.

FRFWe propose that it set standards, connect stakeholders and measure results.

The communityWe propose that it provide access, with clubs developing players.

02 / THE PLAN

13 areas.
One shared goal.

01

Public funding for children and grassroots football

We propose redirecting public funding from professional Superliga teams to children, youth development and community football.

Read the measures
Public money should build the base of the football pyramid. We propose ending public subsidies for the professional first teams in the Superliga and redirecting that funding to children, youth academies, community facilities, women’s and girls’ football, and the training of coaches and referees. Professional teams should cover player wages, transfer fees, agents’ fees and operating deficits through commercial revenue and private funding. Public support for a professional club’s academy must be separately budgeted, transparent and tied to measurable benefits for children; it must not subsidise the first team. The state sets public funding policy and priorities; the Romanian Football Federation certifies sporting standards and measures outcomes. The aim is to develop future players and widen access to football.
02

The active child

Real participation matters more than registration numbers.

Read the measures
An active child participates in at least 30 weeks of organised activity per season, with at least 70% attendance. The pathway: initial contact → trial → registration → regular activity → development → academy → debut → integration into senior football → professional football → transfer. We measure the cost of engaging a child and the actual proportion progressing from school competitions to weekly football.
03

Football close to home

From play to professional football, with an age-appropriate pathway.

Read the measures
U6–U10: PLAY; U11–U13: DEVELOPMENT; U14–U16: TALENT; U17–U19: HIGH PERFORMANCE; U19+: PROFESSIONAL FOOTBALL. Mixed teams at younger ages. Football close to home, with journeys generally around 30 minutes. Saturday and Sunday leagues, without registration at two different clubs. In winter: indoor football and futsal.
04

Open schools and facilities

Put existing facilities to use and cover the real operating costs.

Read the measures
Opening a sports facility involves utilities, heating, cleaning, security, staffing, insurance and administration. The facility activation package provides standard agreements and clear rules. The school benefits package transparently provides sports equipment, minor repairs and training.
05

The people who develop players

Vetted volunteers, supported coaches and an engaged community.

Read the measures
A community team: 10–20 children, 2–3 vetted adults and an affiliated club. Volunteer coaches need verified identity, an integrity certificate, child safeguarding training and a code of conduct. They receive training, equipment, insurance, mentoring and a pathway towards UEFA C, without disguised remuneration. The pilot compares one coordinating coach for 8 teams with one for 12.

Read the training and capacity plan

06

Keeping children safe

Clear safeguarding rules and independent reporting channels.

Read the measures
A safeguarding lead at every youth club and a coordinator at each county football association. Rules covering two-adult supervision, changing rooms, transport, photos and video, communication, accommodation and travel. A separate integrity channel for unofficial payments, favouritism and fraud. An independent channel for abuse and risks to children.

Read the training and capacity plan

07

Developing talent fairly

Progress, education and the opportunity to reach senior football.

Read the measures
Monitor relative age effects and biological maturation. Multiple assessments are used only for major selections. The existing regional U14 talent identification programme selects players for the U15 squad; the proposed local centres complement it by providing regular additional training. Academies are assessed through progress, education, retention, debuts and senior playing minutes.
08

Romanians around the world

Extend and professionalise connections with young players abroad.

Read the measures
We build on the existing FRF registration platform, proposing country coordinators, vetted observers, comparable assessments and support for families. The pathway covers eligibility checks, match observations, technical assessment and invitations decided exclusively by the FRF. Children are observed close to home; travel to Romania requires confirmed funding, including an accompanying adult. The same pathway applies to girls. Identification starts at U13, with structured observation at U14–U19. U21 and senior squads sit outside this network. Published priorities cover eligibility, the sporting window and competition level; distance is used for logistical planning. Chapter 15 details selection, data protection and club relationships; chapter 31 separates this component’s budget from the community pilot.

09

A shared digital system

Track attendance, outcomes and funding through an accessible platform.

Read the measures
A mobile-friendly platform that works with a weak signal, supports audits and data exports, and avoids dependence on a single supplier. It covers identity and consent, squads, attendance, matches, a funding register, safeguarding, reporting concerns and a monitoring dashboard. The funding register tracks: source → programme → beneficiary → expenditure → evidence → outcome.
10

Costs and funding

Transparent assumptions. No contribution from vulnerable families.

Read the measures
Working model at 2026 prices: €420 per occupied place per year in variable costs, plus regional and central coordination, new-place setup and contingency. At 10,000 stable places, the full estimate is €5.28 million per year. Vulnerable families contribute nothing. The full document sets out formulas, potential funding routes and three scenarios for 2030–2040. These are planning estimates, not approved funding.

PEOPLE BEFORE EXPANSION

Developing the people who develop football

Grassroots football grows through the people who make it possible: coaches, referees, safeguarding leads, administrators and volunteers. Team numbers cannot grow without training, mentoring and supervision capacity. We propose a trainer-development programme built with the competent FRF and county association structures, developing people and reorganising existing capacity on the basis of results.

National specialistsRegional trainersLocal practitioners
1. Assess capacity and performance

Before setting the programme’s scale, assess each specialism and county over a 3–5-year reference period agreed before data collection. Map available trainers, qualifications and time commitments; course capacity, costs and geographical coverage; applicants, graduates and active practitioners; community needs and training facilities.

Also measure time to qualification, graduate activity at 12 and 24 months, cost per active practitioner and response times for access or accreditation applications. Record missing data and begin collecting it. Publish the methodology, data limitations and aggregated results.

Findings may lead to curriculum improvements, additional trainer development, simpler procedures and resource reallocation through the competent bodies. Publish performance criteria in advance. Address professional shortcomings through notification, support and a remedial period, followed by reassessment and a reasoned decision with an appeal route. Risks to children and serious misconduct require immediate protective measures without waiting for these stages.

2. Build a national team of specialists

The national team develops the curriculum, prepares regional trainers and monitors quality. Transparently select specialists in Romania, Romanian professionals working in academies and sports organisations abroad and, where needed, international experts. Seek technical support from UEFA, FIFA, federations and academies without presenting unconfirmed partnerships as guaranteed.

The programme strengthens and, where justified by assessment, reorganises existing capacity with the competent FRF and county association structures. Each specialism has its own lead: coaching expertise does not automatically confer expertise in refereeing, safeguarding or administration.

3. Select a new generation of trainers

Recruit through county associations and open applications, with published criteria, selection stages and appeal procedures. Actively attract people aged 20–30 without excluding strong candidates of other ages. Experienced specialists can serve as trainers, mentors and assessors.

Selection considers specialist competence, communication and teaching ability, integrity, role-specific checks, availability for work in the region and a realistic local involvement plan. Nomination by a county association does not guarantee admission.

4. Two pathways: trainers and practitioners

REGIONAL TRAINERS. The working assumption is a 6–12-month pathway adapted to specialism and experience. It may include two national intensive modules in Bucharest or other centres, each lasting up to one–two weeks, alongside remote study, local practice and mentoring. Validate duration and frequency in the pilot and budget for participants’ time and expenses. Assessment includes practical competence and a teaching demonstration. Graduation and authorisation permit teaching within the programme only in the approved specialism and scope.

LOCAL PRACTITIONERS AND VOLUNTEERS. Training duration is proportionate to the role and applicable requirements. Delivery is close to home, including weekends, combining accessible remote theory with practice and field assessment. Graduates undertake only tasks for which they have demonstrated competence, with necessary supervision and mandatory qualifications. They do not automatically gain the right to train or certify others.

Online learning supports theory without replacing practical demonstrations, teaching interaction or field assessment.

5. Distinct specialisms, shared standards of conduct

COACHES: teaching, child development, session planning, inclusion, parent communication and age-appropriate technical skills.

REFEREES: applying the laws of the game, match management, communication, conflict management and incident reporting.

CHILD SAFEGUARDING: risk prevention, recognising concerns, receiving reports, confidentiality and referral to the competent people or institutions. Require the behavioural integrity certificate for roles subject to the legal obligation and explicitly define checks for each category, including volunteers. Checks do not replace training and supervision.

ADMINISTRATORS AND COORDINATORS: organisation, the digital platform, records, budgets, financial transparency and volunteer coordination.

VOLUNTEERS: clear responsibilities, conduct, safety, inclusion and role boundaries, with access to guidance.

Ethics, safeguarding and conduct form a shared core. Specialist training and assessment remain distinct.

6. A controlled cascade with two training transfers

The national team trains and supports regional trainers. Regional trainers prepare and mentor local practitioners. Local practitioners deliver activities in clubs, schools and communities.

Three levels mean two training transfers. Practitioners may later become trainers only after additional preparation, assessment and authorisation. Teaching rights do not pass on automatically. All levels use common, centrally updated curricula, materials and criteria; local adaptations are documented and preserve essential standards.

7. Independent assessment and links to official pathways

Attendance alone is insufficient. Assess knowledge, practical demonstrations, supervised activity and, for trainers, teaching ability. Final assessors are independent of the candidate’s direct trainer; decisions are reasoned and open to appeal.

Distinguish attendance confirmation, competence certification, programme teaching authorisation and the right to assess or award official qualifications. Programme certification does not replace mandatory licences or qualifications. For coaches, seek recognition of eligible components within the official UEFA C pathway under FRF-approved conditions; this is a negotiated objective, not a promised equivalence.

While formal partnerships are pending, prepare materials and offer introductory activities in administration, volunteering and safeguarding with competent partners and within applicable requirements. These activities do not automatically confer official qualifications.

8. Support continued community involvement

Each graduate has a clear local role, a mentor and access to materials, support and ongoing development. Budget for participation and activity expenses. Clearly separate paid roles from volunteering, with transparent responsibilities and conditions.

Young referees receive mentoring at their first matches and clear procedures for reporting and responding to intimidation or violence. Do not judge trainers solely by pass rates or automatically impose repayment obligations on volunteers. Assess practice quality, graduate activity, compliance with standards and community progress. Reassess trainers periodically against published criteria.

9. Pilot, budget and conditions for expansion

Align the first training programme with the community pilot. Candidate numbers and specialisms follow pilot needs and available mentoring and assessment capacity. Schedule training so that essential staff are ready before the activities they lead.

Set the budget after the capacity assessment and present it separately: selection, trainers, travel, accommodation, materials, venues, practice, assessment, coordination and post-course support. Count shared community-pilot costs once only. Secure funding for additional costs before committing to activities; existing pilot estimates are not automatically sufficient for the new component.

Track practical competence, active graduates at 6 and 12 months, observed field quality, cost per graduate and active practitioner, continuity of coaches, referees and volunteers, safeguarding compliance and feedback from children, parents and organisations. Continue monitoring at 24 months where cohorts allow.

Set success thresholds before the pilot. Compare results with each area’s starting point and resources. Do not open more teams than can be safely trained, supported and supervised. Develop the people, demonstrate field capacity, then expand.

06 / ACCOUNTABILITY

Trust is built
on clear rules.

What information is needed before nationwide approval?
Detailed FRF finances; UEFA/FIFA allocations; the distribution of coaches and referees; an inventory of school sports clubs, sports-focused secondary schools and facilities; actual costs for halls, insurance, equipment and transport; family expenses; sponsor and local authority capacity; and a full diaspora audit.
How is public funding tracked?
The funding register tracks the source, programme, beneficiary, expenditure, evidence and outcome. Public development funding serves the community rather than senior player wages, transfers, agents or first-team deficits.
